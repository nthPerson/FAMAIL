\begin{abstract}
% DRAFT (2026-07-11) — pending final results; numbers below are committed headline
% values. % src: PAPER/supply-lift/FINDINGS.md; PAPER/external-metrics/FINDINGS.md;
% PAPER/baselines/demographic-oversampling/FINDINGS.md
Demand models learned by imitation from urban mobility data inherit the
demographic service inequity encoded in their demonstrations. We present
FAMAIL, a fairness-oriented data-augmentation method that \emph{edits}, rather
than regenerates, a small attribution-targeted slice of real taxi
trajectories, then upweights the edited demonstrations so the fairness
survives training. The editor combines two bounded, gradient-guided mechanisms
under one differentiable objective: \emph{trim} relocates pickups out of
over-served hotspots, and \emph{lift} reroutes drivers' final seeking minutes
into under-served areas, with the supply consequences of a lift made
differentiable and endogenous to the objective. Every edit is confined to a
two-cell ball, with a frozen driver-identity discriminator as a realism
guardrail. On two cities (Shenzhen; San Francisco), editing improves
established fairness measures that the objective never optimizes ---
demographic parity, disparate impact, and the Theil index. And where
demand-only editing improves such measures purely by reducing over-service,
the supply channel lift was designed to open adds statistically robust taxi
presence to under-served areas in both cities: the improvement is no longer
pure leveling-down. Upweighted behavior cloning carries the fairness-metric
gains into trained policies edit-specifically --- random-upweighting and
select-the-fairest controls are null --- while a targeted
demographic-oversampling baseline captures only part of the data-level gain
despite fabricating 10.5\% of the corpus, and its untargeted placebo degrades
fairness outright.
\end{abstract}
