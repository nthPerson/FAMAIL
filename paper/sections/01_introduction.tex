Human-generated spatial-temporal data (HSTD), such as taxi trajectories,
passenger trip records, and gig-worker traces, is increasingly used to learn
human decision-making strategies for mobility modeling, simulation, and
downstream decision applications
\cite{zhang2019cgail,zhang2022cgail,feng2020simulate}. However, HSTD is not a
neutral record of human behavior. It captures not only how human agents make
sequential decisions, but also how these decisions collectively shape where
services are repeatedly provided and where they remain scarce. Fairness is
therefore a collective property of the HSTD corpus: it emerges from the way
many local decisions jointly allocate services across locations and
demographic groups. In urban mobility, for example, taxi trajectories encode
both drivers' passenger-seeking strategies and the resulting spatial
distribution of service across neighborhoods, sketched in
Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}. On the Shenzhen corpus we study, the advantaged
district receives $3.0\times$ the taxi service per unit of demand that the
disadvantaged district receives.

Models trained on such data may reproduce not only useful human expertise but
also demographic disparities embedded in the demonstrated service allocation
\cite{zhao2017menshopping,wang2021directional}. Once deployed, these learned
decisions can reinforce historical allocation patterns, propagating bias from
HSTD to downstream applications \cite{ensign2018,lumisaac2016}.

Fairness interventions are conventionally grouped by where they act.
\emph{In-processing} methods constrain the training objective
\cite{zheng2023,lamalfa2026fairppo,zhao2025fairdrlst}. They fix one model. The
demonstrations are left untouched, so the next model trained on the same corpus
inherits the same bias. \emph{Pre-processing} acts on the data itself
and so survives retraining, but the available pre-processing tools were built
for other settings or other targets. Reweighing and related repairs assume
tabular records with labels \cite{kamirancalders2012}, not sequential
demonstrations. Fairness-aware generation synthesizes new data instead of
repairing real records
\cite{xu2018fairgan,vanbreugel2021decaf,hastingsblow2025diffaug}. The one
pre-processing method built for trajectories perturbs them to harden identity
models, not to improve fairness \cite{hu2023stifgsm}. What is missing is a
pre-processing method that edits real trajectories so the corpus is fairer,
while keeping each trajectory faithful to the driver who produced it.

Realizing such an intervention is challenging. First, collective fairness is a
corpus-level property, but edits are local: an editor must trace the disparity
to the trajectories that carry it and identify the local edits with the largest
aggregate effect. Second, reducing disparity does not necessarily improve
disadvantaged-group welfare: an intervention can narrow a group gap by lowering
the advantaged group's measured outcome instead of raising the resources the
disadvantaged group receives. In taxi mobility, reducing the contribution of
trips from heavily served areas narrows the measured service gap without
directing additional taxi presence to under-served neighborhoods. Third, the
intervention must work under a limited edit budget, because modifying a large
portion of HSTD is costly and may distort the human behaviors that make the
data valuable. The few edits it does make must be physically valid, faithful to
the recorded driver, and influential enough to survive downstream training.
Section~\ref{sec:overview} makes these difficulties precise as five labeled
challenges, each answered by one component of our method: the edit budget
(C1), fidelity (C2), the wrong target (C3), leveling up rather than down
(C4), and surviving training (C5).

We address these challenges with FATE, a Fairness-Aware Trajectory
Editing framework for budgeted fairness intervention in HSTD. Given a limited
edit budget, FATE attributes corpus-level disparity to individual trajectories
and applies bounded modifications to those with the greatest estimated
aggregate fairness impact, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}. FATE
distinguishes outcome-side edits, which change the measured allocation
statistic, from resource-aware edits, which alter the underlying
resource-provision process. Because the edited demonstrations are only a small
fraction of the corpus, FATE applies edit-aware weighting to preserve their
influence during downstream training. In our taxi-mobility instantiation,
outcome-side and resource-aware edits correspond to
modifying pickup endpoints and rerouting the final passenger-seeking segments,
respectively. Experiments on real-world HSTD show that FATE consistently
reduces demographic service disparity on measures the objective never
optimizes, increases distinct-taxi presence in
under-served areas, and preserves metric-level fairness improvements in
learned policies. Our contributions are summarized as follows:

\begin{itemize}
\item We formulate fairness mitigation in HSTD as a budgeted
local-to-collective problem: disparity emerges from the aggregate effects of
many sequential decisions, while only a small subset is edited by design.
This formulation connects a bounded edit budget on local demonstrations to 
corpus-level fairness.
\item We develop FATE, which attributes collective disparity to individual 
trajectories and edits them through two channels. We show why outcome-side 
editing alone can reduce measured disparity without improving the 
disadvantaged group's service, and introduce a differentiable resource-aware 
channel that adds it.
\item We instantiate FATE for taxi mobility and evaluate it on real-world HSTD 
from Shenzhen and San Francisco. Editing consistently reduces demographic 
service disparity on measures the objective never optimizes, and increases 
taxi presence in under-served areas. Under edit-aware weighting those 
improvements reach downstream imitation-learned policies at the metric level, 
with random-subset and most-fair controls isolating the edit. We release our 
code and dataset via an anonymous link.\footnote{\url{https://anonymous.4open.science/r/FATE-review}
