Urban mobility policies are increasingly learned by imitation: a model is
trained to reproduce the GPS trajectories generated by real human taxi drivers,
because those trajectories carry operating expertise that no simulator or
hand-built policy provides \cite{zhang2019cgail,zhang2022cgail}.
But the same trajectories also record where the fleet chose to serve,
and a driver working for fares does not serve every neighborhood equally:
service concentrates where pickups are frequent and lucrative, and where that
falls is not independent of local demographics. On the Shenzhen corpus
we study, the advantaged district receives $3.0\times$ the taxi service per
unit of demand that the disadvantaged district receives
(Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}).

A model trained on such a corpus learns the service gap along with the
expertise, because nothing in imitation training distinguishes the two;
models trained on human-sourced data are documented to reproduce, and even
amplify, the biases that data records
\cite{zhao2017menshopping,wang2021directional}. Once deployed, the imitator
re-enacts the bias and further contaminates the very data the next generation 
of models will train on \cite{ensign2018,lumisaac2016}. Repairing this is a
trade-off between two properties of the data. We want the corpus to be
\emph{fairer}, and we want it to keep its \emph{fidelity}: edited or
replacement data must still look like real human driving, or the policy
trained on it is useless. Interventions that buy fairness by giving up
fidelity, or protect fidelity by leaving the data alone, leave the problem
unsolved. That fairness-versus-fidelity trade-off is the central problem our 
approach seeks to address.

Existing interventions act either on the model or on the data, and each
leaves one side of the trade-off open. In-processing methods act on the
model: they add a fairness penalty to its training objective while the
demonstrations stay as recorded
\cite{zheng2023,lamalfa2026fairppo,zhao2025fairdrlst}.
A model can be trained to behave more fairly than its own examples, but the
correction lives in that model and not in the data, so every other model
trained on the same corpus inherits the bias unchanged. Data-side generation
instead synthesizes a fairer dataset outright, with generative networks
constrained to remove the protected signal
\cite{xu2018fairgan,vanbreugel2021decaf} or with diffusion models whose
synthetic samples improve downstream fairness \cite{hastingsblow2025diffaug}.
Generation pays for fairness with fidelity: the synthetic trajectories are
samples from a model rather than records of human driving, and nothing
guarantees they remain drivable routes or plausible seeking behavior.
Data-side reweighing changes how often existing trajectories are seen
during training, and repair methods adjust recorded features in place
\cite{kamirancalders2012,feldman2015}; both rebalance what is represented,
but neither can create taxi service in neighborhoods where the recorded
fleet seldom went.

Achieving both at once is hard for five concrete reasons; they are why the
families above fall short, and each dictates a component of our method:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{C1: the data is scarce.} Real demonstrations are expensive
and irreplaceable, so discarding the biased slice or regenerating the
corpus discards the asset: the repair must \emph{edit the data we have}.
\item \textbf{C2: edits must preserve fidelity.} A fairness edit is
worthless if the result no longer reads as real driving; every edit is
therefore bounded and gated by a frozen driver-identity discriminator that
must still recognize the original driver (\S\ref{sec:objective}).
\item \textbf{C3: equal service is the wrong target.} A business district
should out-serve a quiet block; the right target is the service variation
that demand does \emph{not} explain, FATE's objective $F_{\mathrm{demo}}$
(\S\ref{sec:objective}).
\item \textbf{C4: fairness must level up, not down.} A gap can close while
the under-served gain nothing; demand-only editing demonstrably behaves
this way here (\S\ref{sec:leveling}), so the editor needs a supply channel
that adds real taxi presence where it is missing (\S\ref{sec:editor}).
\item \textbf{C5: the gains must survive training.} Standard imitation
training averages a tenth-of-corpus edit away; the edited demonstrations
must be upweighted, and the recovered fairness traced to the edits rather
than to the upweighting itself (\S\ref{sec:downstream},
\S\ref{sec:experiments}).
\end{itemize}

FATE (Fairness-Aware Trajectory Editing) answers these challenges as data
augmentation for imitation learning. It keeps the real corpus and edits its
most-biased slice in place, selecting trajectories by an exact attribution
of the fairness deficit (C1). Each edit is a bounded relocation of a
recorded pickup, or a bounded reroute of a driver's final seeking minutes,
small enough that the frozen identity discriminator
\cite{ren2020stsiamese} still recognizes the driver (C2). Edits follow the
gradient of a differentiable objective whose primary term is the
demand-adjusted fairness $F_{\mathrm{demo}}$ (C3) and whose supply channel
rewards moving real taxi presence into under-served neighborhoods (C4).
The edited demonstrations are then upweighted during behavior cloning,
instance reweighing \cite{kamirancalders2012} transplanted to
demonstrations, so the corpus's fairness survives training (C5). The gains
at the corpus level do not depend on which model later consumes the data;
the training-survival claim we demonstrate with behavior cloning, the
simplest and most widely used imitation family, and extending it to
adversarial imitation is future work.\footnote{Code (anonymized for double-blind
review): \url{https://anonymous.4open.science/r/FATE-review}.

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A fairness-aware trajectory editor.} Bounded edits to real
trajectories, chosen by attribution and gated by a frozen
driver-iden\-tity discriminator, optimizing de\-mand-ad\-justed demographic
fairness (C1--C3; \S\ref{sec:method}).
\item \textbf{A structural diagnosis of de\-mand-only editing.} It
improves measured fairness while the under-served gain nothing; we show
why, and why a differentiable supply channel is the remedy (C4;
\S\ref{sec:leveling}).
\item \textbf{A transfer recipe with edit-specificity controls.} Upweighted
behavior cloning carries the corpus's fairness into trained policies;
random-subset and most-fair controls isolate the edits as the cause (C5;
\S\ref{sec:experiments}).
\item \textbf{Two-city validation on never-optimized measures.} The full
protocol runs on Shenzhen and San Francisco trajectories, scored on
external fairness measures (demographic parity, disparate impact, the
Theil index) that the objective never optimizes (\S\ref{sec:experiments}).
\end{itemize}
