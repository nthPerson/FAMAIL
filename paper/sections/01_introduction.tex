Demand models for urban mobility are increasingly learned by imitation:
policies are trained to reproduce the trajectories a real fleet actually
drove \cite{zhang2022cgail}. But trajectories carry more than behavior.
They record the fleet's service allocation --- which neighborhoods drivers
sought passengers in, and which they passed over --- and in real cities
that allocation tracks who lives where: housing prices, incomes, migrant
population share. A model that imitates such data faithfully learns the
inequity as competence, and a model deployed to guide dispatch re-enacts
it, sending supply where supply already went --- the feedback loop
documented for other data-driven allocation systems
\cite{ensign2018,lumisaac2016}.

Interventions typically target one end of the pipeline or the other.
In-processing methods regularize the model while leaving the
demonstrations biased \cite{zheng2023}, so the objective and the training
signal pull against each other at every step. Generative repair replaces
the data wholesale, but rewriting an entire distribution risks trading
realism for the fairness score and makes any improvement hard to attribute
to anything in particular. FAMAIL takes a third position. As \emph{data
augmentation}, it keeps the real corpus, edits the small slice of it where
attribution says the unfairness lives, and generates nothing. At most $k$
trajectories are edited ($k = 10{,}000$ on the primary city, about a tenth
of the corpus), each perturbed within a two-cell ball under a frozen
driver-identity discriminator, so realism is inherited from the data
rather than re-learned. The edited demonstrations are then
\emph{upweighted} during imitation --- instance reweighing
\cite{kamirancalders2012} transplanted to demonstrations --- so the
fairness of a small slice survives training instead of being averaged
away.

The subtlety is not measuring unfairness but repairing it honestly.
FAMAIL's first editing mode, \emph{trim}, moves only demand: it relocates
recorded pickups within over-served areas, and on the primary city it
improves measured fairness --- by \emph{leveling down}. Every
one of the 2{,}455 pickups it edits originates in an advantaged cell, and
the under-served group's service never moves; the classic leveling-down
objection to equalization \cite{parfit1997,mittelstadt2024} applies
literally.

We show this behavior is structural --- the constrained optimum of any
demand-only editor on this objective (\S\ref{sec:leveling}) --- and that
diagnosis dictated the second mode. \emph{Lift} reroutes drivers' final
seeking minutes into under-served areas and makes the supply consequences
of the reroute differentiable and endogenous to the objective: the editor
is rewarded for providing service where it improves fairness, not merely
for redistributing demand. Together, trim and lift raise demand-adjusted
demographic fairness on Shenzhen by $+0.0226$

and --- for the first time in our experiments --- add statistically
robust taxi presence to the under-served group, raising its mean service
ratio by $+0.0176$ under the optimizer's fractional-presence accounting
(tier-1) and by $+0.0411$ when supply is recounted as distinct taxis from
raw GPS (tier-2).

Neither edit survives training on its own. The edited slice is a tenth of
the corpus, and a vanilla behavior-cloning objective averages it away ---
a genuine null we verify rather than assume. Upweighting the edited
demonstrations recovers the fairness with a monotone dose-response, and
two controls pin the recovery to the edit itself: neither upweighting an
equal-sized random subset nor upweighting the corpus's already-fairest
trajectories reproduces it. Because a fairness method scored on its own
objective proves little, evaluation rides on established measures the
objective never optimizes --- demographic parity, disparate impact
($+0.0162$ on the primary city's migrant axis), and the Theil index ---
computed before and after editing.

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A trim+lift trajectory editor.} Two bounded editing modes,
gradient-guided under one differentiable objective, with the supply
consequences of edits made endogenous to it, and driven by two exact
attribution mechanisms: demand deficit attribution localizes existing
unfairness, and supply-gradient attribution localizes the value of added
taxi presence.
\item \textbf{A structural diagnosis of leveling down.} Demand-only
editing closes fairness gaps only from the top; we show why --- selection,
leverage, and frozen supply --- and show that a differentiable supply
channel is the non-perverse remedy.
\item \textbf{A transfer recipe with edit-specificity controls.}
Upweighted imitation carries the data-level fairness into trained
policies, and random-upweighting and select-the-fairest controls isolate
the edit as the cause.
\item \textbf{Two-city validation on never-optimized measures.} The full
protocol runs on Shenzhen and San Francisco and is scored on external
fairness measures the objective never optimizes, against a
demographic-oversampling baseline on the primary city.
\end{itemize}
