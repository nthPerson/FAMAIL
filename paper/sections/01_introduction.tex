Urban mobility policies are increasingly learned by imitation: models are
trained to reproduce the trajectories real humans actually
drove \cite{zhang2022cgail}. But trajectories carry more than behavior.
They record the fleet's service allocation --- which neighborhoods drivers
sought passengers in, and which they passed over --- and in real cities
that allocation tracks demographics: housing prices, incomes, migrant
population share. Models that imitate such data reproduce the inequity and
re-enact it when deployed --- a feedback loop documented in other
data-driven allocation systems \cite{ensign2018,lumisaac2016}.

Interventions typically target one end of the pipeline or the other.
In-processing methods regularize the model while leaving the demonstrations
biased \cite{zheng2023}, so objective and training signal conflict.
Data-generation and data-rebalancing methods instead alter the
demonstrations --- synthesizing fair data
\cite{xu2018fairgan,vanbreugel2021decaf} or reweighing and resampling them
\cite{kamirancalders2012} --- but this can reduce realism, shift the
distribution, and obscure the source of any fairness gain.
Figure~\ref{fig:teaser} illustrates the gap: the demonstrations are worth
preserving, yet a policy that imitates them unchanged learns the service gap
they record.

FATE takes a third position. As \emph{data augmentation}, it keeps the real
corpus, edits a small slice of its most unfair trajectories, and generates
nothing. At most $k$ trajectories are edited ($k = 10{,}000$ on the primary
city, about a tenth of the corpus), each perturbed within a two-cell radius
under a frozen driver-identity discriminator (ST-SiameseNet)
\cite{ren2020stsiamese}, so realism is inherited from the data rather than
re-learned. The edited demonstrations are then \emph{upweighted} during
imitation --- instance reweighing \cite{kamirancalders2012} transplanted to
demonstrations --- so the edited slice's fairness survives training instead
of being averaged away.

FATE's first editing phase, \emph{trim}, moves only demand: it relocates
recorded pickups within over-served areas, improving measured fairness on the
primary city without helping the under-served group at all. Every one of the
2{,}455 pickups it edits originates in an advantaged cell and not one lands in
a disadvantaged cell, so the gap closes only by reducing over-service ---
analogous to the classic leveling-down objection to equalization
\cite{parfit1997,mittelstadt2024}, though here service is relocated rather
than removed.

This behavior is structural --- the constrained optimum of any demand-only
editor on this objective (\S\ref{sec:leveling}) --- and that diagnosis
dictated the second phase. \emph{Lift} reroutes drivers' final seeking
minutes into under-served areas and makes their supply consequences
differentiable and endogenous to the objective: the editor is rewarded for
providing service where it improves fairness, not merely for redistributing
demand. Together, trim and lift raise demand-adjusted demographic fairness on
Shenzhen by $+0.0226$

(\S\ref{sec:exp-data-level}).

Neither edit survives training on its own. The edited slice is a tenth of
the corpus, and a vanilla behavior-cloning objective averages it away ---
a genuine null we verify rather than assume (\S\ref{sec:exp-downstream}). Upweighting the edited
demonstrations recovers the fairness with a monotone dose-response, and
two controls pin the recovery to the edit itself: neither upweighting an
equal-sized random subset nor upweighting the corpus's already-fairest
trajectories reproduces it. Because a fairness method scored on its own
objective proves little, evaluation rides on established measures the
objective never optimizes --- demographic parity, disparate impact, and the
Theil index --- computed before and after editing; all three improve on the
primary city, every interval excluding zero (\S\ref{sec:exp-data-level}).

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A fairness-aware trajectory editor.} Two bounded editing phases
under one differentiable objective that makes the supply consequences of
edits endogenous, each driven by an exact attribution mechanism
(\S\ref{sec:attribution}).
\item \textbf{A structural diagnosis of demand-only editing.} Demand-only
editing closes fairness gaps only from the top; we show why --- selection,
leverage, and frozen supply --- and that a differentiable supply channel is
the non-perverse remedy.
\item \textbf{A transfer recipe with edit-specificity controls.}
Upweighted imitation carries the data-level fairness into trained policies,
with random-upweighting and most-fair selection controls isolating the edit
as the cause.
\item \textbf{Two-city validation on never-optimized measures.} The full
protocol runs on Shenzhen and San Francisco, scored on external fairness
measures the objective never optimizes, against a demographic-oversampling
baseline on the primary city.
\end{itemize}
