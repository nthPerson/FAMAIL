\textbf{Fairness interventions in machine learning.} Fairness interventions
are conventionally grouped by where they act: pre-processing methods repair
the training data, in-processing methods constrain the learner, and
post-processing methods adjust its outputs \cite{barocas2023}. FATE belongs
to the pre-processing family, whose canonical instruments --- instance
reweighing \cite{kamirancalders2012} and disparate-impact repair of feature
distributions \cite{feldman2015} --- were built for labeled classification
data. Among formal fairness definitions \cite{vermarubin2018}, FATE targets
conditional statistical parity \cite{corbettdavies2017}: disparities count
only after permitted factors are adjusted for. As formulated, none operate on
sequential demonstrations or measures fairness over a spatial service
distribution --- the setting this paper requires. Both instruments reappear
here in unfamiliar roles: reweighing
applied to edited demonstrations rather than labeled examples, and the parity
condition transplanted from a classifier's predictions to a continuous
spatial service ratio and made a differentiable editing objective.

\textbf{Fairness in urban transportation.} Transportation equity has its own
measurement tradition, in which the Gini index over service distributions is
the most widely used instrument \cite{horchergraham2021,karner2024},
alongside entropy- and welfare-based indices
\cite{theil1967,atkinson1970,demaio2007}. The closest modeling neighbor
regularizes a ride-hailing demand predictor at training time, shrinking a
racial prediction-error gap from 0.361 to 0.084 \cite{zheng2023}.
% verified: paper/reviews/2026-07-12-prose-citation-audit.md (zheng2023 Table 2, Conv-LSTM arm)
That correction, however, leaves the demonstrations biased: it lives inside
one model's loss and does not transfer to any other consumer of the data.
FATE addresses the same bias one stage earlier --- on the demonstrations the
model trains on, rather than inside its loss --- and is scored on equity
measures its objective never optimizes.

\textbf{Imitation learning for mobility, and mobility as identity.}
Imitation learning is an established route to urban mobility models:
conditional GAIL variants recover taxi-driver strategies from trajectories
\cite{zhang2019cgail,zhang2022cgail}, explainable extensions expose the
recovered policies \cite{pan2020xgail}, and neural simulators generate human
mobility \cite{feng2020simulate} --- but this literature optimizes realism, and
none audits or repairs the demographic fairness of the imitated allocation. A parallel line establishes that individual mobility is
highly identifying: trajectory-user linking recovers who generated a trace
\cite{gao2017tuler,zhou2018tulvae,miao2020deeptul}, and Siamese verification
decides whether two seeking traces share a driver \cite{ren2020stsiamese}.
FATE contributes no new generator or imitation algorithm; it edits the
demonstrations such models consume, and repurposes the identity-verification
line as a frozen realism guardrail, avoiding the instability of a live
adversarial game \cite{hoermon2016}.

\textbf{Adversarial perturbation and recourse.} The editing machinery
descends from adversarial examples: bounded signed-gradient perturbations
\cite{goodfellow2015fgsm,kurakin2017ifgsm} instantiated spatio-temporally
\cite{hu2023stifgsm}, with discrete structure bridged by straight-through
estimation \cite{bengio2013ste} and continuous
relaxation \cite{jang2017gumbel,maddison2017concrete}. Algorithmic recourse showed that the same
counterfactual tooling can prescribe beneficial change rather than attack
\cite{ustun2019recourse,wachter2018counterfactual}, but only to flip one
individual's decision --- with no population-level fairness target and no
obligation to preserve a data distribution's realism. FATE extends that
constructive reading from the individual to the population: the bounded
perturbation edits real trajectories to reduce demographic service inequity,
and the $\varepsilon$-bound is reinterpreted from an attack-stealth budget to
one for preserving driver identity.

\textbf{Leveling down and feedback loops.} Two critiques frame what a
fairness gain should mean. The leveling-down objection --- equality achieved
by making the better-off worse rather than the worse-off better --- originates
in the ethics of equality \cite{parfit1997} and has been applied to
machine-learning fairness constraints \cite{mittelstadt2024}, with data
augmentation observed to be the one intervention class that helps the
disadvantaged group instead \cite{zietlow2022}. Feedback-loop analyses show
that allocation systems trained on their own historical output entrench a
self-reinforcing bias \cite{ensign2018,lumisaac2016} --- in our domain, the
continued under-service of already-underserved neighborhoods. Both critiques
apply here: our demand-only editor \emph{trim} only manipulates
over-service, an analogue of leveling down (\S\ref{sec:leveling}); the
supply channel \emph{lift} exists expressly to level up, rerouting real
taxi presence into under-served neighborhoods; and because demand recorded
where service is thin understates true need, demand endogeneity bounds
what any demand-adjusted metric can see.
