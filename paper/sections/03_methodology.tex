\section{Methodology}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Problem Formulation}\label{sec:problem}

We study taxi service allocation on a city discretized into $0.01^{\circ}$ grid
cells $g$ and hourly time blocks $t$; a pair $i=(g,t)$ with recorded activity is
an \emph{active unit}, and $N$ denotes their count ($N \approx 34{,}500$ on the
primary city). Each active unit carries a demand $D_i$ --- its recorded
pickups --- and a supply $S_i$ --- active-taxi presence, aggregated over the
$5{\times}5$ cell neighborhood to match how a cruising taxi is visible to nearby
demand. Service received relative to demand is the \emph{service ratio}
$Y_i = S_i / \max(D_i, d_0)$, where the demand floor $d_0 = 0.5$ prevents
near-empty cells from producing unbounded ratios.
% src: PAPER/argument/02_datasets.md (0.01° grid, hourly blocks);
% src: PAPER/supply-lift/LIFT_ALGORITHM_REFERENCE.md §3 (floors, 5×5 presence); §4.1 (N = 34,524 active units, Shenzhen)

Each cell additionally carries demographic covariates $x_i$ describing its
neighborhood: housing price, per-capita compensation, and migrant population
share. These covariates resolve at district granularity --- about ten distinct
profiles on the primary city --- which bounds the resolution of any demographic
analysis built on them (\S\ref{sec:objective}).
% src: PAPER/argument/02_datasets.md

The data itself is a corpus $\mathcal{T}$ of real per-driver trajectories, each
a sequence of passenger-seeking states ending in a pickup. The trajectories,
not the aggregate grids, are the editable objects: $D$ and $S$ are
deterministic aggregations of $\mathcal{T}$, so moving where a driver seeks and
picks up moves demand and supply with it.

\textbf{Task.} Given a corpus $\mathcal{T}$ whose service allocation is
demographically inequitable, produce an edited corpus $\mathcal{T}'$ that
(i) is measurably fairer; (ii) remains realistic at the level of individual
trajectories --- edited trajectories must remain recognizable, in aggregate,
to a frozen driver-identity discriminator as their original drivers; and
(iii) transfers its fairness into a policy trained on it by imitation. FATE
is a \emph{data-augmentation} method: it edits a small, attribution-targeted
slice of the real trajectories --- at most $k$ of them ($k = 10{,}000$ on the
primary city), each perturbed within an $L_\infty$ ball of radius
$\varepsilon = 2$ grid cells --- and generates nothing.
% src: LIFT_ALGORITHM_REFERENCE.md §3 (EPSILON_BALL, budget)
The edited slice is then upweighted during downstream training so that its
fairness is not averaged away (\S\ref{sec:downstream}).

\subsection{The Fairness Objective}\label{sec:objective}

FATE edits trajectories by gradient ascent on a single differentiable
objective that combines demographic fairness, spatial equity, and realism.

\textbf{Demand-adjusted demographic fairness ($F_{\mathrm{demo}}$, primary).}
Raw parity across neighborhoods is the wrong target: taxi service legitimately
tracks demand, and a business district \emph{should} see more pickups than a
quiet residential block. We therefore measure fairness only in the component of
service that demand does not explain --- the logic of conditional statistical
parity \cite{corbettdavies2017}, which restricts disparities to those not
explained by permitted factors. Stage one fits a flexible power basis $g_0(D)$
from per-unit demand to the service ratio and takes the residual
$R_i = Y_i - g_0(D_i)$; stage two regresses that residual on the demographics
and asks how much they explain. We evaluate stage two in closed form rather
than through an explicitly fitted regression: with $X$ the z-scored
demographic matrix, $\tilde{X} = [\mathbf{1}\; X]$ its design matrix,
$H = \tilde{X}(\tilde{X}^{\top}\tilde{X})^{-1}\tilde{X}^{\top}$ the \emph{hat
matrix} of that regression --- the projection onto the column space of
$\tilde{X}$ \cite{hoaglinwelsch1978} --- and $M$ the centering matrix,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fdemo}
F_{\mathrm{demo}}
\;=\; \frac{R^{\top}(I-H)\,R}{R^{\top}M\,R}
\;=\; 1 - r^{2}_{\mathrm{demo}} .
\end{equation}
Eq.~\eqref{eq:fdemo} is the stage-two regression in closed form: the
numerator is its residual sum of squares, the denominator its total; $H$ is
constant, so each edit step implicitly re-fits the regression exactly. As a
ratio of quadratic forms in $R$ it is smooth, so exact gradients flow from
the fairness score through $Y$ to the coordinates being edited, and the
idempotence of its projections underwrites the exact per-unit attribution of
\S\ref{sec:attribution}. The quantity $r^{2}_{\mathrm{demo}}$ is interpretable
as the share of demand-adjusted service inequity attributable to demographics;
the Frisch--Waugh--Lovell exactness behind that reading and the $O(N)$
evaluation identity are given in Appendix~\ref{app:derivations}.
Boundary cases orient the scale: $R \in \operatorname{span}(X)$ gives
$F_{\mathrm{demo}} = 0$ (fully unfair), and $R \perp X$ gives $1$ (fully
fair).
% src: PAPER/objective-motivation/MOTIVATION.md; PAPER/argument/03_fairness_theory.md

Two caveats are load-bearing. First, $F_{\mathrm{demo}}$ --- \emph{demo}
for demographic dependence --- is an \emph{associational} quantity: the
partial $R^2$ of a cross-sectional regression on observational
district-level demographics, with no identification and no counterfactual.
With about ten district profiles the association is ecological,
and we make no individual-level claims. Second, adjusting for demand treats
recorded demand as exogenous; but demand recorded where service has
historically been thin is itself suppressed by that under-service. We return
to this in \S\ref{sec:leveling}, where the same assumption turns out to bound
what a demand-only editor can achieve.
% src: PAPER/objective-motivation/MOTIVATION.md (associational caveat; demand endogeneity)

\textbf{Spatial equity ($F_{\mathrm{spatial}}$, secondary).} Independent of
demographics, service should also be spread evenly relative to the taxi supply
actually present. We track two supply-normalized service rates per active
unit --- the \emph{demand-service ratio} $\mathrm{DSR}_i = D_i / S_i$
(pickups per unit of taxi presence) and the \emph{arrival-service ratio}
$\mathrm{ASR}_i = A_i / S_i$ (dropoffs per unit of taxi presence), with
$A_i$ the unit's recorded dropoffs and $D_i$, $S_i$ as in
\S\ref{sec:problem} --- and score the inequality of each with the Gini
index, an inequality measure widely used in transportation equity
research \cite{horchergraham2021,karner2024}. The Gini enters as a
pairwise mean absolute difference, differentiable almost everywhere
and hence usable inside the gradient objective:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fspatial}
\begin{split}
\mathrm{Gini}(x) &\;=\; \frac{\sum_{i}\sum_{j}\,\lvert x_i - x_j\rvert}
{2N^{2}\,\bar{x}},\\
F_{\mathrm{spatial}} &\;=\; 1 - \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(\mathrm{Gini}(\mathrm{DSR})
+ \mathrm{Gini}(\mathrm{ASR})\bigr).
\end{split}
\end{equation}
Being demographic-independent, the term acts as a
spatial smoothness regularizer complementing $F_{\mathrm{demo}}$.
% src: PAPER/argument/03_fairness_theory.md (F_spatial definition)
% src: famail_temporal/fairness/spatial.py (DSR/ASR ratios; sorted-identity evaluation, no N² materialization)
% src: famail_temporal/docs/RESEARCHER_HANDOFF.md §4 (DSR/ASR acronym expansions)

\textbf{Realism guardrail ($F_{\mathrm{fidelity}}$).} An edit that improves
fairness by producing trajectories no real driver would generate is useless
for training a realistic imitation model. Because individual mobility signatures
are highly identifying --- the premise of the trajectory-user-linking
literature \cite{gao2017tuler,zhou2018tulvae,miao2020deeptul} --- we score
realism with a frozen driver-identity discriminator of the ST-SiameseNet
family \cite{ren2020stsiamese}: an edited trajectory that still reads as its
driver has preserved human fidelity. The discriminator is trained once and
never updated during editing, avoiding the instability of a live adversarial
game \cite{hoermon2016}. Under FATE's small bounded edits the discriminator's
gradient is near zero, so it acts as a realism \emph{guardrail}, not a driver
of edits; identity-axis stability under both phases is confirmed empirically
(\S\ref{sec:experiments}). Distributional collapse is monitored separately at
evaluation time with a Jensen--Shannon check over aggregate mobility statistics
\cite{feng2020simulate}.
% src: PAPER/objective-motivation/MOTIVATION.md (F_fidelity guardrail framing)

\textbf{Scalarization.} The three terms combine linearly,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
\mathcal{L} \;=\; \alpha_{\mathrm{sp}} F_{\mathrm{spatial}}
+ \alpha_{\mathrm{ca}} F_{\mathrm{demo}}
+ \alpha_{\mathrm{fi}} F_{\mathrm{fidelity}},
\qquad \alpha = (0.1,\; 0.8,\; 0.1).
\end{equation}
% src: PAPER/objective-motivation/weight-sensitivity/ (EXTENDED_FRONTIER.md + DECISION.md, 2026-07-11)
The weights are selected \emph{empirically}: a sweep of the weight simplex
(fidelity fixed at 0.1), scored across all three metric classes
(\S\ref{sec:exp-setup}), selects $(0.1, 0.8, 0.1)$ by a three-class
criterion --- maximize $\Delta F_{\mathrm{demo}}$ subject to
$\Delta F_{\mathrm{spatial}} \ge 0$ \emph{and} the supply-channel lift-up
remaining significant under both accounting tiers. Two properties make the
choice robust rather than delicate: the primary gain is insensitive to the
weights over a wide range, and the fidelity gradient is dormant under
bounded edits --- the discriminator constrains, but does not steer, the
optimization. The full sweep, with the flatness and monotone-lift-up
evidence behind the criterion, is reported in \S\ref{sec:exp-robustness}.
% src: PAPER/objective-motivation/weight-sensitivity/EXTENDED_FRONTIER.md (flatness, monotone lift-up, criterion)

\subsection{Attribution: Localizing the Deficit and the Remedy}\label{sec:attribution}

The objective is optimized one trajectory at a time, so FATE must decide
which trajectories are worth a slot in the edit budget. It uses two
attribution mechanisms, one per editing phase (\S\ref{sec:editor}).
\emph{Demand deficit attribution} locates where existing unfairness
concentrates, and drives the demand-side phase; \emph{supply-gradient
attribution} locates where added taxi presence would most improve fairness,
and drives the supply-side phase. The first attributes the deficit; the
second, the remedy.

\textbf{Demand deficit attribution (drives trim).} Because $M$ and $I-H$ are
idempotent, the explained share $r^{2}_{\mathrm{demo}}$ of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:fdemo} admits an exact per-unit partition of the fairness
deficit across active units (Appendix~\ref{app:derivations}), with a signed
variant that separates over- from under-served units. The trajectories
whose pickups land in the highest-deficit units are the ones trim edits.
% src: PAPER/argument/03_fairness_theory.md (per-cell attribution)

\textbf{Supply-gradient attribution (drives lift).} The second mechanism asks,
of every active unit: \emph{how much fairer would the city's service be with
marginally more taxi presence here, at this hour?} The quantity that answers
it is the gradient of the objective with respect to supply, evaluated at the
city's current state. To obtain it, we add an auxiliary
\emph{supply-perturbation} input $\Delta S \in \mathbb{R}^{N}$ --- one entry
of hypothetical added taxi presence per active unit --- evaluate every
supply-dependent term at $S + \Delta S$, and read
$\partial \mathcal{L} / \partial S_i$ by automatic differentiation at
$\Delta S = 0$: the marginal fairness effect of added presence at each of the
$N \approx 34{,}500$ active units, in one backward pass. (The same $\Delta S$
pathway later carries the real supply consequences of lift edits,
\S\ref{sec:editor}.)
The result is a \emph{value-of-presence} map over the city: large where
added presence would most repair the fairness terms --- units starved of
supply relative to their demand and demographic profile --- and small or
negative where presence is already abundant. The map's
$F_{\mathrm{demo}}$ component has a closed form, given with its autograd
verification in Appendix~\ref{app:derivations}.
% src: PAPER/supply-lift/LIFT_ALGORITHM_REFERENCE.md §4.1 (autograd-verified closed form)

To turn the map into candidates, a linearized-offset screen ranks
trajectories whose tails could bend into high-value cells: every trajectory
long enough to carry a movable tail (${\approx}80$k of the corpus's
${\approx}95$k) is scored by its best bounded tail translation
(Appendix~\ref{app:editor}), so the screen favors trajectories that already
nearly serve the right areas, bending real behavior rather than inventing
it.
% src: PAPER/supply-lift/data/oracle.json (n_trajectories_len_ge3 80,427 of n_trajectories_total 95,297)
The screen only \emph{nominates}: every selected
trajectory is subsequently re-optimized individually under the full objective
(\S\ref{sec:editor}), which derives the actual move. In assembling the edit
budget, trim's selection takes precedence and lift fills the remaining slots
with positive-score nominees.
% src: PAPER/supply-lift/LIFT_ALGORITHM_REFERENCE.md §4.2–4.3 (screen; plan assembly)

\subsection{Why Demand-Only Editing Cannot Help the Under-Served}\label{sec:leveling}

Relocating pickups is a demand-side lever, and on its own it improves the
fairness metrics in a specific, troubling way: it redistributes service among
better-served areas while the under-served group gains nothing. On the
primary city, every one of the 2{,}455 pickups edited by the demand-only
editor originated in advantaged cells and not one landed in a disadvantaged
cell; the under-served group's service was untouched, and the demographic gap
closed only from the top.
% src: PAPER/external-metrics/LEVELING_DOWN_MECHANISM.md (2,455/2,455 flow analysis; pre-supply-lift doc) — corroborated at alpha*: PAPER/supply-lift/data/a10/shz_trimonly_a10_external_fairness.json (mean(Y|disadv) 7.0734 -> 7.0734 exactly)
The pattern is analogous to the classic leveling-down objection to
equalization \cite{parfit1997} --- measured equality improves while the
worst-off gain nothing --- recently formalized for machine-learning fairness
together with a prescription to level \emph{up} instead \cite{mittelstadt2024};
notably, data augmentation has been observed to be the one intervention that
helps the disadvantaged group rather than pulling others down
\cite{zietlow2022}. The analogy is inexact in one instructive way: trim
\emph{relocates} pickups under conservation rather than removing service, so
nothing is destroyed --- yet the under-served group is not helped at all,
which is precisely the objection's force.

The behavior is the constrained optimum of the demand-only problem, not an
optimizer artifact, for three compounding structural reasons. First,
selection: demand deficit attribution (Eq.~\eqref{eq:unit-attr}) concentrates in
over-served, high-residual cells, so under-served cells are never edit
candidates. Second, leverage: with supply frozen,
$\partial Y_i / \partial D_i = -S_i / D_i^{2}$ makes padding demand into
high-supply advantaged cells roughly $32\times$ more consequential for $Y$
than removing it from starved ones --- and 93\% of disadvantaged units sit at
the demand floor $d_0$, where removing demand changes nothing.
% src: PAPER/external-metrics/LEVELING_DOWN_MECHANISM.md; LIFT_ALGORITHM_REFERENCE.md §1 (32x; 93%)
Third, the inequity itself is on the supply side --- median taxi presence is
1.8 in disadvantaged cells against 17.6 in advantaged ones --- and a
demand-only editor cannot touch supply. A greedy oracle (an unconstrained
search that upper-bounds what any editor could achieve) closes the remaining
escape route: the only demand-side way to raise the under-served group's
service ratio is to delete its recorded pickups, which is perverse.
% src: LIFT_ALGORITHM_REFERENCE.md §1 (median presence 1.8 vs 17.6; oracle perversity)

The deeper cause unifies the editor's limitation with the metric's. Recorded
demand is not exogenous: where service has historically been thin, demand is
suppressed and latent --- the feedback-loop pathology documented for other
public-allocation systems \cite{ensign2018,lumisaac2016}. The same censored
demand that hides inequity from a demand-adjusted metric leaves a demand-only
editor nothing to move in under-served areas.

This sets up the editor's second phase. The one non-perverse lever is the
numerator: $\partial Y_i / \partial S_i = 1 / \max(D_i, d_0) > 0$ everywhere
--- at the demand floor, $\Delta Y = 2\,\Delta S$ --- so raising under-served
service requires adding taxi \emph{presence}, that is, rerouting real seeking
behavior into under-served cells. %Before building this mechanism we bounded
%its headroom with a greedy oracle, which cleared a pre-registered go/no-go
%threshold on the supply channel alone (\S\ref{sec:experiments}).

\subsection{The Trim+Lift Editor}\label{sec:editor}

Figure~\ref{fig:overview} illustrates the two editing phases on a stylized
city grid.

\textbf{Shared machinery.} Both editing phases repurpose bounded adversarial
perturbation \cite{goodfellow2015fgsm,kurakin2017ifgsm}, in its
spatio-temporal instantiation \cite{hu2023stifgsm}, \emph{constructively} ---
in the spirit of algorithmic recourse
\cite{ustun2019recourse,wachter2018counterfactual}, where the same
counterfactual-perturbation tooling that finds a minimal input change to flip
a decision instead prescribes a minimal, bounded, beneficial change. Per
iteration, the edit offset takes a signed-gradient step under a cumulative
$L_\infty$ clip,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:step}
\delta \leftarrow \mathrm{clip}\bigl(\eta \cdot
\mathrm{sign}(\nabla_{\delta}\,\mathcal{L}),\, -\varepsilon,\, \varepsilon\bigr),
\qquad
\delta_{\mathrm{total}} \leftarrow
\mathrm{clip}(\delta_{\mathrm{total}} + \delta,\, -\varepsilon,\, \varepsilon),
\end{equation}
so no pickup ever moves more than $\varepsilon = 2$ cells from its recorded
location, regardless of iteration count --- the $\varepsilon$-bound
reinterpreted as an identity-preservation budget rather than an attack-stealth
budget. The discrete grid is bridged by a temperature-annealed soft cell
assignment (Appendix~\ref{app:editor}).
% src: PAPER/objective-motivation/MOTIVATION.md (editor); PAPER/argument/03_fairness_theory.md

\textbf{Trim.} The demand-side phase: pickups selected by demand deficit
attribution relocate within the $\varepsilon$-ball, padding recorded demand
into over-served, supply-rich cells so their measured over-service falls
(\S\ref{sec:leveling}). Its optimization path is exactly the demand-only
editor's.

\textbf{Lift.} Trajectories nominated by supply-gradient attribution have
their whole seeking tail translated: the pickup moves by the full offset
$\delta$, earlier tail states move by linearly tapered fractions, and the
anchor state before the tail never moves --- so the trajectory physically
cruises through the under-served area before picking up rather than
teleporting into it. The moved states carry their supply with them,
differentiably, contributing presence to the supply perturbation $\Delta S$
over the same $5{\times}5$ neighborhood. Supply is thus \emph{endogenous}: the
optimizer sees the supply consequences of its own moves and is rewarded for
providing service where it improves fairness, not merely for redistributing
demand. The fidelity term scores the actual rerouted tail at every iteration,
and each completed edit updates a shared running state, so later edits
optimize against the city as earlier edits left it. The taper weights,
per-state presence mass, supply floor, and the
$\mathrm{clamp}(S + \Delta S,\, s_0)$ evaluation are given in
Appendix~\ref{app:editor}.

\textbf{Physical validity.} Edits must satisfy the same king-move rule the
source trajectories are filtered on --- consecutive states move at most one
cell per axis --- enforced by an exact backward-reachability repair detailed
in Appendix~\ref{app:editor}. Infeasible edits are not applied: lift skips
them in-loop, and the small fraction of trim edits with no compliant repair
(about 5\%) is reverted to the originals post hoc under the same rule --- a
rule adopted before its metric effects were computed (\S\ref{sec:experiments}).
% src: PAPER/supply-lift/data/a10/shz_a10_metrics.json (n_reverted_infeasible_trim 118/2,455 ≈ 4.8%);
% PAPER/supply-lift/FINDINGS.md §8 (rule-first disclosure)

\textbf{Budget and phase order.} Trim retains its selection by demand
deficit attribution; lift fills the remaining budget with positive-score
nominees ---
on the primary city, $k = 10{,}000$ splits into 2{,}455 trim and 7{,}545 lift
edits --- and the supply gradient is computed on the post-trim state.
% src: LIFT_ALGORITHM_REFERENCE.md §4.3, §5 (budget split, phase order)
The two-phase structure is a scientific control, not an algorithmic
necessity. Freezing trim keeps its optimization identical to the demand-only
editor, which buys two properties: demand-only results reproduce exactly
inside the combined run, and the trim-only versus trim+lift comparison is a
clean ablation in which every difference is attributable to the new
mechanism. A unified single-pass editor --- supply endogenous everywhere, the
gradient choosing each edit's character --- is a natural extension we leave
as future work.
% src: docs/presentations/meeting_42_update/trim_plus_lift_explainer.md §3; LIFT_ALGORITHM_REFERENCE.md §14

\subsection{Downstream Recipe: Upweighted Imitation}\label{sec:downstream}

Editing alone is necessary but not sufficient. The edited slice is small by
design --- roughly a tenth of the corpus ---
% (derived: 10,000 / 95,297 ≈ 10.5%; src: PAPER/baselines/demographic-oversampling/FINDINGS.md corpus count)
and a vanilla behavior-cloning
objective, weighting every demonstration equally, averages it away and
relearns the original bias; we verify this null empirically
(\S\ref{sec:experiments}). FATE therefore \emph{upweights} the edited
demonstrations in the imitation loss --- instance reweighing for fairness
\cite{kamirancalders2012}, a member of the pre-processing family alongside
disparate-impact repair \cite{feldman2015}, transplanted to imitation
learning. Whether the recovery is specific to the \emph{edit}, rather than an
artifact of oversampling or of selecting fair data, is tested against two
controls --- upweighting a size-matched random subset, and upweighting the
corpus's fairest trajectories --- with outcomes reported in
\S\ref{sec:experiments}. This intervention targets, on the training data, the
same demonstration bias that in-processing regularization has addressed at the
model level \cite{zheng2023}; FATE moves the intervention to the
demonstrations themselves.
% src: PAPER/objective-motivation/MOTIVATION.md (downstream pairing); PAPER/argument/04_evaluation.md §1 (controls)
