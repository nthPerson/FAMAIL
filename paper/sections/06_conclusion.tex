constrained model or a synthetic corpus; the fairness it repairs is
collective, a property of the whole corpus's service allocation.

It has two
stages: a budgeted, attribution-directed edit of a small slice of real
trajectories under one bounded, fidelity-guarded objective, then an
edit-aware weighting of the edited demonstrations so the fairness survives
imitation. On two cities
the edits improve established fairness measures the objective never
optimizes, and, unlike the demand-only editor whose gains never reached the
under-served group, the supply channel adds statistically significant taxi
presence to that group. Because the edited slice is small, uniform weighting
averages it away. Upweighted behavior cloning carries those gains into
trained policies, and the control arms confirm the edit itself is responsible.

The claims come with stated bounds. The optimized fairness term is
associational: a partial $R^2$ over roughly ten district-level demographic
profiles, with no identification and no individual-level reading.
Adjusting for recorded demand treats demand as exogenous, but demand is
suppressed exactly where service has been historically thin, so a
demand-adjusted metric under-detects the inequity it audits: the same
censoring that bounds any demand-side editor. With five or six paired
seeds, no single test survives multiple-comparison correction; the one
comparison extended to twelve paired seeds in each city (the flagship
w30 recovery) does ($p = .00049$, 12/12 positive in both), and the
remaining downstream evidence rests on effect direction and magnitude,
confidence intervals, a monotone dose-response, and null controls rather
than on any uncorrected p-value. San Francisco reproduces the Shenzhen
conclusions with no algorithm change, but its baselines are city-specific and
its magnitudes are not comparable. And the fidelity guardrail certifies that
edited trajectories still read as their drivers: identity preservation,
not fine-grained trajectory-shape realism.

Three directions follow. The first is the unified single-pass editor
\S\ref{sec:leveling} anticipates, with supply endogenous for every edit. The
second is transfer: whether other imitation objectives and adversarially
trained generators inherit the edited corpus's fairness as upweighted behavior
cloning does, and whether training-side allocation constraints can close the
rollout allocation gap that data editing alone only attenuates
(Appendix~\ref{app:results}). The third is the budget: $k$ is configured, not
swept, here, so how collective fairness varies with it is unmeasured.
